Module 0

What is PARSE and why do you need it

The 93% vs 22% gap. The ICRC hack. What the protocol covers.

93% of humanitarian professionals already use AI tools like ChatGPT and Claude for work. But only 22% of organizations have any AI governance policy (HLA & DFS, 2025). This means sensitive patient data from conflict zones is being processed through commercial LLM APIs with no safeguards.

The ICRC hack

In January 2022, a cyberattack on the ICRC exposed personal data of over 515,000 vulnerable people, many searching for missing family members. If it can happen to the most security-conscious humanitarian organization, it can happen to anyone.

PARSE addresses three interdependent problems: confidentiality (patient data carries extreme risk if exposed), coordination (data flows through multiple actors and jurisdictions), and fairness (conflict-zone data is subject to bias mechanisms absent from standard literature).

What can go wrong
"I have a 34-year-old pregnant woman from northern Rakhine, Rohingya, with P. falciparum malaria and sickle-cell trait..."
A field nurse types this into ChatGPT to get treatment advice. Every word is now stored on a server she does not control. If it leaks, the patient's ethnicity, location, and medical condition are exposed. In a genocide context, this is a threat to life.
Check your knowledge

A nurse uses ChatGPT to translate a patient's symptoms from Arabic to English. She types the patient's name, age, village, and symptoms. What is the minimum risk level?

Tier 3 at minimum (individual patient data with direct identifiers). If the village is in an active conflict zone or the symptoms suggest conflict-related injury, it escalates to Tier 4. The name alone makes it Tier 3 regardless of context.
Module 1

The data polarization

Too little vs too much. When both extremes lead to the same failure.

Humanitarian health data exists at two extremes. Understanding which one you're in determines everything else.

Too little data

Acute emergencies, paper tally sheets, unknown denominators. The risk: invisibility. What you don't count doesn't get resourced.

Too much data

47 dashboard indicators, weekly reports nobody reads. The risk: false confidence. Acting on numbers that look precise but carry hidden distortions.

Both extremes share the same failure: the gap between having information and knowing what to do with it.

Too little
A mobile clinic near a frontline sees 12 patients per day. No lab, no X-ray, paper records. The nurse knows malaria is rising but has no denominator. She can't tell coordination how bad it is because she has no population estimate.
Too much
A data scientist at a coordination center manages data from 15 villages and 2 cities. 15 years of data, 200 variables, 47 spreadsheets. Everyone keeps sending more. She still doesn't know what to do with it.
Check your knowledge

Your DHIS2 dashboard shows 312 cholera cases across 8 facilities. Three facilities are in areas accessible only during ceasefires (~10 days/month). Is the total likely an overestimate or underestimate?

Underestimate. The inaccessible-during-fighting days are when populations are most exposed and least able to seek care. The cases you don't see are probably more, not fewer. This is a "too little data" problem hidden inside a dashboard that looks complete.
Module 2

Missingness: MCAR, MAR, MNAR

Why conflict data is almost always Missing Not At Random.
TypeMeaningIn conflict data
MCARMissing completely at random. A nurse skips a field by accident.Rare
MARMissing at random, predictable from other variables. Men report weight less.Sometimes
MNARMissing because of the value itself. Sickest patients die before arrival.The default
Why this matters

Standard imputation methods (mean imputation, MICE) assume MAR. Applied to MNAR data, they produce confident but wrong estimates: narrow confidence intervals around biased point estimates. You become certain of the wrong answer.

MNAR in practice
A hospital in eastern DRC reports 0 cases of sexual violence for three consecutive weeks. The hospital was operational. Staff were present.
The zero is informative. Survivors may not be reaching the facility, may not feel safe disclosing, or the security situation may prevent travel. Zero reported cases ≠ zero cases. This is classic MNAR: the value is missing because of what the value would have been.
Check your knowledge

You're building a malnutrition model from facility data. 30% of records are missing the MUAC (mid-upper arm circumference) measurement. You use mean imputation to fill them. What's the problem?

Mean imputation assumes MAR. But MUAC is most likely missing for the sickest children - the ones who died before measurement, were too weak to extend their arm, or arrived at night when the trained measurer wasn't present. Replacing their values with the mean makes severe malnutrition disappear from the data. Use sensitivity analyses instead.
Module 3

Five biases you won't find in any textbook

Survivorship, topology, non-stationarity, MNAR, the protected-attribute paradox.

Standard algorithmic fairness assumes data exists, protected attributes are known, the process is stationary, and missingness is MAR. In conflict, none of this holds.

1. Survivorship / Access bias

Only people who reached your facility are in the data. The sickest, most remote, most besieged are systematically absent.

2. Conflict-topology bias

Front lines, checkpoints, and siege create non-random partitions. The partition correlates with conflict exposure. You see one side, not the other.

3. Temporal non-stationarity

A hospital is bombed. A population is displaced overnight. Models trained on pre-event data are invalid. The model won't tell you it's wrong.

4. MNAR as the default

Every category of missing data is missing for reasons correlated with the values that would have been observed.

5. The protected-attribute paradox

The most fairness-relevant attributes (ethnicity, tribal affiliation) are too dangerous to collect. You cannot audit for fairness across a variable you cannot measure.

Check your knowledge

You are auditing an AI triage tool across three facilities in an ethnic conflict. You want to check if the tool performs equally for all ethnic groups. What is the fundamental problem?

Protected-attribute paradox. Recording ethnicity could endanger patients. You can't audit fairness across a variable you can't safely collect. The protocol suggests auditing at facility level as a proxy, since different facilities likely serve different populations due to conflict-topology bias.
Module 4

The six-stage bias chain

From collection to decision: how biases compound at each stage.

Bias is not one problem. It is a chain. Each stage inherits and amplifies the distortions from the one before.

0
Pre-existing
Healthcare already unequal before conflict. Structural exclusion.
1
Collection
Only survivors who reach facility are counted. Survivorship, self-selection, gender bias.
2
Digitization
Paper to digital transfer, forced categories. Transcription and structured-field bias.
3
Transmission
Only connected facilities report regularly. Connectivity-driven selection.
4
Analysis
Models assume stability that doesn't exist. Stationarity, MNAR, denominator bias.
5
Decision
Dashboard numbers treated as truth. Anchoring, automation bias, invisibility bias.
The terminal bias

Resources flow toward documented need, which follows data quality, which follows infrastructure access, which follows safety. End result: the safest populations get the most resources. The most endangered get the least.

Check your knowledge

A coordination team allocates emergency nutrition supplies based on DHIS2 malnutrition admissions. Three well-connected urban facilities report 400 cases. Five rural clinics with intermittent connectivity report 60 cases total. Where does most of the supply go?

Urban facilities get more. But the rural clinics likely serve a larger, more vulnerable population with worse malnutrition. Their low numbers reflect transmission bias (Stage 3) and survivorship bias (Stage 1), not lower need. The bias chain turns data quality into a resource allocation mechanism that punishes the most affected.
Module 5

Using LLMs safely: three architectures

Sensitivity tiers, de-identification standard, the decision flowchart.

The first question is never which model. It is what the model is allowed to see.

Sensitivity tiers

Tier 0-1

Public/operational data. Commercial APIs OK.

Tier 2

Aggregate health data. Zero-retention API only. Small denominator test.

Tier 3

Patient data. NEVER external. Local models only.

Tier 4

Protection-sensitive. NEVER transmit. Encrypt at rest.

Three architectures

Architecture A (preferred)

LLM builds tools, templates, code. You run them locally. AI never sees real data. Works for all tiers.

Architecture B

LLM sees de-identified/aggregated data. Zero-retention API. Tiers 0-2 only. Requires de-identification standard.

Architecture C

Local open-weights model. Data never leaves your network. All tiers, but needs hardware and validation.

Architecture A in action
"Write me an R script that takes a CSV with columns 'age_group', 'sex', 'diagnosis', 'outcome' and produces a cohort analysis of TB treatment outcomes."
The LLM sees variable names and logic. Field staff run the script on real DHIS2 data locally. The model never touches a patient record.
Check your knowledge

You want to use an LLM to summarize clinical notes from 50 patients to prepare a medical handover report. You have no local model. What architecture should you use?

Architecture A. Clinical notes are Tier 3 (individual patient data). You cannot send them to an external API (rules out B). You have no local model (rules out C). So you use Architecture A: ask the LLM to generate a template for the handover report, then fill it in manually with the patient data. The LLM helps with structure, not content.
Module 6

NLP and topic modelling for humanitarian orgs

What machines can read that you don't have time to. Reporting bias detection.

Topic modelling takes a collection of documents and automatically discovers groups of words that appear together. Applied to MSF's published reports from conflict zones, it reveals reporting bias: which medical themes dominate communications and which are systematically absent.

This is Architecture A in practice

The pipeline processes only public text (published reports, situation updates). No patient data is involved. The LLM never sees anything above Tier 0.

What reporting bias looks like

In an analysis of 500+ MSF reports across 12 conflict zones:

Theme% of reportsStatus
Trauma/Surgery54%Well-reported
Infectious disease52%Well-reported
Mental health37%Moderate
Chronic disease14%Under-reported
Disability9%Under-reported

Under-reported themes receive less donor attention, less funding, and less programmatic investment. The silence in organizational communications becomes a gap in care.

The silence is the signal
NLP cannot tell you what is happening on the ground. But it can tell you what your organization is saying - and what it is not saying.
Check your knowledge

Your topic model shows that "mental health" appears in 37% of reports overall, but only 13% of reports from Ukraine and 19% from Myanmar. What might explain this?

Several possibilities, and you cannot distinguish between them from the text alone: (1) mental health services may genuinely be less available in those contexts, (2) reporting culture may prioritize trauma/surgery over mental health, (3) stigma may suppress disclosure, (4) report authors may assume mental health is implied and not mention it explicitly. The topic model flags the pattern; a field epidemiologist interprets it.
