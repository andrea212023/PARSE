Introduction

Why PARSE exists

93% of humanitarian professionals already use AI tools like ChatGPT and Claude for work. Organizational AI governance stands at 22%. In January 2022, a cyberattack on the ICRC exposed personal data of over 515,000 vulnerable people, many searching for missing family members in conflict zones.

PARSE closes this gap. It is a protocol and training framework for the responsible use of large language models and data analysis tools in humanitarian health settings, calibrated to the specific bias mechanisms of conflict-zone health data.

The ICRC hack
Data from the Restoring Family Links programme was accessed by hostile actors. If this can happen to the most security-conscious humanitarian organization, it can happen to anyone. Every additional system your data touches multiplies the attack surface.

The data polarization

Humanitarian health data exists at two extremes. Understanding which one you're in determines everything else.

Too little data

Acute emergencies, paper tally sheets, unknown denominators. The risk is invisibility: what you don't count doesn't get resourced.

Too much data

47 dashboard indicators, weekly reports nobody reads. The risk is false confidence: acting on numbers that look precise but carry hidden distortions.

Both extremes share the same failure: the gap between having information and knowing what to do with it.

Part I

Data sensitivity tiers

Not all data requires the same protections. Your data's tier determines which AI architecture you can use.

Tier 0: Public aggregate

Published reports, aggregate counts. LLM use unrestricted.

Tier 1: Operational

Supply chains, logistics. APIs permitted after stripping facility locations.

Tier 2: Aggregate health

Case counts, attack rates. Zero-retention API only. Small-denominator test required.

Tier 3: Patient data

Clinical records, lab results. Never transmit to external APIs. Local models only.

Tier 4: Protection-sensitive

Facility locations in active conflict, ethnicity, sexual violence, combatant-status injuries. Never transmit externally. Access-logged, encrypted at rest.

Context-dependent sensitivity

The same data can change tiers based on context. Malaria case counts in a stable programme: Tier 1. Malaria case counts in a region where ethnic targeting follows disease-prevalence lines: Tier 4. When in doubt, classify one tier higher.

Three safe architectures for LLM use

The first question is never which model. It is what the model is allowed to see.

Architecture A

LLM builds tools. You run them locally. AI never sees real data.

Architecture C

Local open-weights model. Data never leaves your network.

All tiers ✓ (needs hardware)

De-identification standard (for Architecture B)

Before transmitting data to any external LLM service:

StepAction
1Remove all direct identifiers (names, DOB, phone numbers)
2Small-denominator test: suppress any cell with n < 15
3Strip geographic precision below district level (20,000+ population)
4Reduce temporal precision (week numbers, not exact dates)
5Motivated-intruder test: could someone with local knowledge re-identify a person?
6Document the de-identification steps in a log

Prohibited uses

Regardless of architecture: never use LLMs to make autonomous clinical decisions; never classify patients by ethnicity, tribal affiliation, or combatant status; never use LLM outputs as evidence without independent verification; never generate synthetic patient data without expert review.

Part III

Missingness: MCAR, MAR, MNAR

TypeMeaningIn conflict data
MCARMissing completely at random. A nurse skips a field by accident.Rare
MARMissing at random, predictable from other variables. Men report weight less.Sometimes
MNARMissing because of the value itself. Sickest patients die before arrival.The default
Why this matters
Standard imputation methods (mean imputation, MICE) assume MAR. Applied to MNAR data, they produce confident but wrong estimates: narrow confidence intervals around biased point estimates. You become certain of the wrong answer.
Concept check

Your cholera surveillance shows 312 cases from 8 facilities. Three facilities are in areas accessible only during ceasefires (~10 days/month). Is the total case count likely to be an overestimate or underestimate?

Underestimate. The inaccessible-during-fighting days are when populations are most exposed and least able to seek care. The cases you don't see are probably more, not fewer.

Five conflict-specific biases

Standard algorithmic fairness doesn't cover these. They are unique to health data from conflict zones.

1. Survivorship / Access bias

Your data only includes people who reached your facility. The sickest, most remote, most besieged are systematically absent. Any facility-based estimate underestimates the true burden.

2. Conflict-topology bias

Front lines, checkpoints, and siege create non-random partitions of the population into observable and unobservable groups. The partition correlates with conflict exposure.

3. Temporal non-stationarity

A hospital is bombed, a population is displaced overnight. Models trained on pre-event data are invalidated instantly. The model won't tell you it's wrong.

4. MNAR as the default condition

Every category of missing data is missing for reasons correlated with the values that would have been observed. Standard imputation methods produce confident wrong answers.

5. The protected-attribute paradox

The most fairness-relevant attributes (ethnicity, tribal affiliation, displacement status) are too dangerous to collect. You cannot audit for fairness across a variable you cannot measure.

Concept check

You are auditing an AI triage tool across three facilities in an ethnic conflict. You want to check if the tool performs equally for all ethnic groups. What is the problem?

Protected-attribute paradox. Recording ethnicity could endanger patients. The protocol suggests auditing at facility level as a proxy, since different facilities likely serve different populations due to conflict-topology bias.

The six-stage bias chain

Bias is not one problem. It is a chain. Each stage inherits and amplifies the distortions from the one before.

StageWhat happensKey bias
0. Pre-existingHealthcare already unequal before conflictStructural exclusion
1. CollectionOnly survivors who reach facility are countedSurvivorship, self-selection, gender
2. DigitizationPaper to digital transfer, forced categoriesTranscription, structured-field bias
3. TransmissionOnly connected facilities report regularlyConnectivity-driven selection
4. AnalysisModels assume stability that doesn't existStationarity, MNAR, denominator
5. DecisionDashboard numbers treated as truthAnchoring, automation, invisibility
The terminal bias
Resources flow toward documented need, which follows data quality, which follows infrastructure access, which follows safety. End result: the safest populations get the most resources. The most endangered get the least.

Fairness audit checklist

Before deploying any analytical tool on humanitarian health data:

CheckQuestion
AccessWhich populations are and are not represented?
TopologyDoes the analysis span different accessibility zones?
TemporalDoes the data span a major conflict-phase change?
MNARIs missingness likely informative? Sensitivity analysis included?
AttributesAre there fairness-relevant attributes that cannot be collected?
StructuralWho designed the question? Who validated outputs? Does value return to the field?
DenominatorCould any output enable re-identification?
ValidityWhat events would invalidate this analysis?

The tool catches explicit identifiers but not subtle re-identification risks. It cannot run the motivated-intruder test: that requires a human who knows the local context. The tool is a first filter, not a replacement for judgment.

NLP and topic modelling

Topic modelling takes a collection of documents and automatically discovers groups of words that appear together. Applied to MSF's published reports from conflict zones, it reveals reporting bias: which medical themes dominate communications and which are systematically absent.

This is Architecture A in practice. The pipeline processes only public text. No patient data is involved.

Under-reported themes receive less donor attention, less funding, and less programmatic investment. The silence in organizational communications becomes a gap in care. NLP cannot tell you what is happening on the ground. But it can tell you what your organization is saying, and what it is not saying.
